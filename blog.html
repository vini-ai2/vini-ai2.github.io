2026 · embedded systems · 6 min

The mixer wasn't wrong. The sign was.

Our drone would arm, spool up, and immediately try to flip itself over. Not crash — correct itself into a crash, with real conviction. That's the signature of a control loop that's technically working: the PID output looked reasonable on the serial monitor, gains were sane, the IMU wasn't obviously lying. Which meant the bug wasn't in the controller. It was in what happened to the controller's output on the way to the motors.

Where it was hiding

The mixer takes throttle, roll, pitch, and yaw correction and distributes them across four motors with a sign pattern that depends on motor position and spin direction. Get one sign backwards and the motor doesn't ignore the correction — it actively fights it, in exactly the direction that makes the tilt worse. On our layout, two of the four signs were flipped, so any small disturbance became self-reinforcing instead of self-correcting. It looked like an unstable controller. It was an unstable mixer.

Fixed by re-deriving the mixer matrix from the actual motor layout instead of copying one from a reference build with a different frame orientation, then bench-testing each motor's correction direction by hand before ever spinning props.

Two more, while I was in there

The ESCs were being driven at 20 kHz when the protocol expected a 50 Hz update rate — a copy-paste from a different ESC family that happened to boot without throwing an obvious error. And the MPU-6050's complementary filter needed its blend constant retuned; too much weight on the gyro term and the attitude estimate drifted slowly over a flight, too much on the accelerometer and it got jittery on every vibration.

None of these three bugs looked like each other from the outside. All three showed up as "the drone won't hold level." The lesson that stuck: when a control system misbehaves, check the plant before you touch the controller.

2026 · computer vision · 5 min

Two coordinate spaces, one very confused face

We were running DECA live off a webcam feed, and after a few seconds the reconstructed face would stop reacting — the mesh kept tracking head pose, but every expression flattened out, like the model had simply given up on emoting. The obvious suspect was the expression encoder itself. It wasn't.

Crop drift, not model drift

DECA crops the face tightly before encoding it, and our crop was being recomputed every frame from the previous frame's landmark estimate rather than a fresh detection. Small errors in that crop compounded frame over frame until the crop window drifted just far enough that expression-relevant detail — the corners of the mouth, the eye region — fell outside it. The identity and pose estimates stayed stable because they depend on coarser features. The expression code collapsed because it doesn't have that luxury.

The second bug was worse to find

Once expressions came back, the mesh projected slightly off from the video — close enough to look like jitter, wrong enough to be obviously wrong on a still frame. That turned out to be a mismatch between two of DECA's output coordinate spaces: code downstream was treating a set of vertices as though they were already in the weak-perspective camera space, when they were still in the model's canonical space. No error, no NaN, just a consistently wrong projection that any single frame made look plausible.

Fixed by tracing the actual transform chain DECA applies internally rather than trusting variable names, and re-running the crop from a fresh FAN landmark detection every frame instead of propagating the old one. Slower per frame, correct every frame — worth it.
